SECTION 1 — SYSTEM OVERVIEW, CONSTRAINTS AND SAFETY

1.1 What the device is

Crimson Veil MK1 is a prehospital internal-bleeding detector. An
eight-electrode ring is placed on the abdomen; a small AC current is injected
through one pair, the resulting voltage is measured on other pairs, and the
host reconstructs a conductivity map. Blood pooling raises local conductivity,
so a map differenced against a baseline shows where bleeding is likely.

The clinical gap is timing. CT and MRI are hospital-only, FAST ultrasound needs
a trained operator, and heart rate and blood pressure are late indicators. MK1
is therefore local and battery-powered: a Raspberry Pi 3 runs the UI and
reconstruction, a 3.5" SPI panel is the display, a Witty Pi 4 BMS manages the
battery, and an ESP32 owns the analog front end. Target runtime is about six
hours from a 6000 mAh cell.

MK1 is an engineering prototype. It is tested on a saline phantom, not on
people or animals, and it makes no clinical claim.

1.2 The measurement

Each shot uses four roles: drive+, drive−, sense+, sense−. Current is injected
through the drive pair and voltage is measured across a different, disjoint
sense pair. This is tetrapolar measurement. Contact impedance sits in the drive
loop and drops out of the sensed voltage, which is the main reason for this
choice over a bipolar (two-electrode) measurement.

The drive pair is adjacent and the sense pair is rotated. For n electrodes the
firmware uses drive (e, e+1) and sense (e+2, e+3), (e+3, e+4), ... for n−3 pairs
per drive position. Eight electrodes give 8 × 5 = 40 points per frame, which is
what the protocol, solver and tests expect.

The AD5933 returns one complex value per point at one frequency. A complex
calibration factor K converts raw data S to impedance: K = Z_expected × S_cal,
then Z = K / S_raw. K corrects magnitude and phase together. The bench firmware
derives K from a known 0.1 µF capacitor; the production firmware applies it.

Reconstruction is difference EIT. Absolute conductivity varies with patient,
contact and lead dress, so the device captures a plain-saline baseline and
reconstructs the difference to the target. Differencing cancels drift and
first-order contact error and avoids needing a patient-specific model. The
baseline is invalidated when the frequency changes, and the kiosk has a CALIB
page to recapture it.

1.3 The signal chain

  AD5933 VOUT
    -> C17 10 µF / R17 10 k high-pass
    -> mirrored Howland (U5A/U5B TL082, 47 k bridge, 5 k current-set,
       0.1 µF coupling)
    -> Drive+ / Drive− -> 4× CD74HC4067 mux bank -> electrode ring
    -> Sense+ / Sense− -> AD620 -> R4 10 k -> AD5933 VIN

The AD5933 contains the frequency generator, 12-bit ADC and I2C interface, so
no separate DDS or ADC is needed. At 5 V it outputs 3.00 V p-p biased at 2.24 V.
Its VIN is the virtual ground of the receive stage, which is why the AD620
reaches it through 10 k.

C17 and R17 block the AD5933's DC bias and give the floating drive network a
defined DC return. Without that return the AD620 bias current walks the inputs
to a rail. The return is on the drive side because a return on Sense+ would add
part of the drive voltage to the sensed signal.

The mirrored Howland turns the AD5933's voltage into a load-independent
current. Two TL082 halves drive Drive+ and Drive− in opposition, giving a
differential drive and about twice the swing for the same rails. The four 47 k
bridge resistors are equal, so the Howland balance condition holds. R8 and R13
set the transconductance: two 10 k parts in parallel, 5 k, giving about
0.6 mA p-p from 3 V p-p. The first schematic used 47 k there; LTspice showed
that gave only 26.5 µA p-p, so the value changed.

The mux bank is four CD74HC4067 16:1 multiplexers. All four see the same
electrode lines, but each common is one role: drive+, drive−, sense+, sense−.
Address bits are shared; each mux has its own active-low enable. That lets any
electrode take any role with four address lines and four enables. The firmware
drives four address bits (GPIO 25, 26, 27, 23) so 16 electrodes can be
addressed; with 8 electrodes the top bit is zero.

The sense pair goes to an AD620. Gain is G = 1 + 49.4 k / RG, with RG a 50 k
trimmer: about 2 at 50 k and 10.9 at 4.95 k. REF sits at 2.5 V from a 47 k/47 k
divider buffered by the spare TL082. The AD620 gives high input impedance and
common-mode rejection, so the sense electrodes are not loaded.

Power is ±5 V; a MAX1044 charge pump makes −5 V from +5 V. The ±5 V rails give
the TL082 and AD620 headroom around the 2.24/2.5 V bias. The AD5933 runs from
+5 V, which is why the bias is 2.24 V, not 1.65 V.

Why these parts:

- AD5933 over AD5940: integrated DDS/ADC/I2C on a cheap breakout. The AD5940
  has better resolution but costs several times more and needs SPI and a more
  complex board. The ~100 kHz ceiling is the trade-off.
- AD620 over AD623: the build uses ±5 V, so AD623's single-supply advantage does
  not apply; AD620 has the better common-mode rejection.
- TL082 over OPA2277: cheap DIP dual JFET op-amp with enough bandwidth and slew
  rate for 3 V p-p at 50 kHz. The earlier docs name OPA2277; the built schematic
  uses TL082.
- Mirrored Howland over single-ended: differential drive doubles the swing for
  the same rails and keeps current symmetric about the bias point.

The calibration fixture is a 0.1 µF capacitor between Drive+ and Drive−, with
Sense strapped to the same nodes. At 50 kHz that is 31.83 Ω at −90°, a stable
reference for magnitude and phase. The hwtest firmware calibrates on the first
half of its samples, verifies on the second, and writes cal.json. The production
firmware loads it, picks the nearest calibrated frequency, and refuses to scan
if it is missing or invalid.

1.4 Data path

The ESP32 owns hardware configuration, the scan sequence and calibration. The
host sends no plan. A scan command runs the sequence once and returns one
frame; continuous scanning is a host policy, so the ESP32 stays deterministic
and single-shot.

The protocol is line-delimited JSON over USB CDC at 115200. On boot and on
identify, the ESP32 sends hello with firmware version, electrode count,
electrode list and sweep frequency. A frame carries points tagged n, f, re, im,
where n is the index in the firmware sequence; the host validates and re-orders
them. Telemetry carries ESP32 and AD5933 temperatures every five seconds.
Errors are a first-class message. JSON was chosen because it is readable in a
serial terminal, MicroPython has it built in, and one object per line gives a
clean resync point.

On the Pi, a Python service owns one ScanSession holding the hardware and solver
backends. Scans and baseline captures are single-flight; blocking work runs in
a worker thread. Continuous mode scans, then waits 60 s while active and 120 s
when idle, with a 30 s activity timeout. The service also runs a five-part
self-test, broadcasts metrics every five seconds, and can upload firmware by
closing the serial port and running mpremote.

Reconstruction is pyEIT's adjacent protocol and back-projection solver, on a
96 × 96 map, encoded as PNG. The firmware and pyEIT can represent a sense pair
in either orientation, so the solver maps each measurement and negates the real
value when the pair is reversed. A baseline must match the target's frequency,
electrode count and point count.

The kiosk is Rust/eframe. It connects to the service over a localhost WebSocket,
decodes the PNG into a texture, and draws the map, electrode ring, readouts and
status as vectors at 15 fps idle, about 30 fps while animating. The layout is
phone-shaped because the panel is 480 × 320.

For work without hardware, a simulated backend, stub solver and mock ESP32
firmware run the whole pipeline on a laptop. The mock uses FEM-generated tables
for 4, 8 and 16 electrodes and cycles an anomaly through eight positions.

1.5 Safety

The safety quantity is current through the body. The design ceiling is 1 mA peak
(2 mA p-p). LTspice drives 1.99 mA p-p into 1 kΩ from 2 V p-p, which is the
ceiling met exactly. The built values — 3 V p-p and 5 k — give about 0.62 mA p-p,
or 0.31 mA peak, so the build runs below the ceiling with the 1 mA case kept as
the verified bound.

The electrodes see 3 V p-p differential. Rails are ±5 V, but the current-setting
resistors and Howland feedback hold the load current inside the envelope. The
AD5933 output is AC-coupled, so there is no DC path through the body; R17
provides the single DC return.

Power is battery or isolated USB only. Nothing is mains-connected while
electrodes are attached, because a mains-referenced return path is the one
failure that makes a low-voltage circuit hazardous. Testing is on 0.9% saline.
There is no patient isolation, defibrillation protection or certification in
MK1; those are explicit non-goals of the prototype.

One build risk: a CD74HC4067 at 5 V wants V_IH near 3.5 V, but the ESP32 drives
3.3 V. Run the mux at 3.3 V or level-shift it.

1.6 Constraints and trade-offs

Cost drove the build to a generic AD5933 breakout, DIP parts and breadboard/
perfboard: about $25 against $80–120 for the ADI board and $150–200 for AD5940
hardware. A KiCad schematic exists, but the PCB has no footprints, so no board
has been laid out; that waits until the analog chain is proven.

Complexity drove three simplifications: one excitation frequency per scan rather
than a sweep (the AD5933 cannot reach 300–500 kHz anyway), eight electrodes as
default rather than sixteen (eight matches the literature ring; sixteen doubles
the mux and electrode hardware), and a Pi/ESP32 split because Linux is not
real-time enough for the AD5933/mux sequence while the Pi is right for the UI
and pyEIT.

The scope is a prototype. No bioimpedance device is approved for prehospital
bleeding detection, and published evidence is animal and phantom work. The
deliverable is a working prototype plus evidence, not a clinical claim.

1.7 What was done, simulated, and left open

Built and committed:

- v0.7 four-part case: parametric OpenSCAD, STLs, drawings, render script and a
  139-check verify.py that ray-casts the exported meshes. Holds the Pi, Witty
  Pi, LCD, LEDs and switches; battery in a separate enclosure.
- MicroPython firmware: AD5933 driver, mux bank, adjacent scan sequence, JSON
  protocol, calibration loading, self-test, telemetry and mock mode.
- Bench firmware (hwtest, MicroPython and Arduino versions) for bringing up the
  AD5933 and analog channel with a known capacitor and writing cal.json.
- Python suite: hardware/solver interfaces, sim hardware, stub solver, pipeline,
  pyEIT solver, PNG encoder.
- Control service: WebSocket protocol, single-flight ScanSession, continuous
  mode, metrics, self-test, firmware upload.
- Rust/egui kiosk, Pi kiosk setup (Plymouth, Xorg/openbox, SPI panel, fbcp,
  systemd unit), and the pytest suite on the simulated backend.

Simulated in LTspice:

- Howland in DC, transient, AC and load sweep: established the 1 mA ceiling,
  output swing, load independence, and that the as-drawn 47 k current-set
  resistors were far too large.
- Full front end with a lumped body model: established gain range, headroom and
  the need for a DC return.
- Contact sweeps: sensed voltage changes about 1.7% from 100 Ω to 1 kΩ and about
  15% at a dry 4.7 kΩ, quantifying the tetrapolar advantage.

Not yet done:

- No PCB laid out or fabricated; the KiCad PCB file is empty.
- Production firmware has not run against the real mux bank and electrode ring
  here; only the mock path and bench hwtest have been exercised.
- No recorded full eight-electrode phantom scan with the production chain.
- 16-electrode hardware exists in firmware and mock tables but has not been
  built.
- Mux logic levels, AD5933 RFB, AD620 common-mode range and Howland resistor
  tolerance still need bench confirmation.
- No human or animal testing, and no clinical validation.
